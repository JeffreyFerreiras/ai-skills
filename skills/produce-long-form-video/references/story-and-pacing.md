# Story and pacing for long-form lessons

Plan what the viewer will understand and see change before polishing the stage.
Use the user's topic, audience, sources, and style. Long-form here means a complete
multi-minute explanation with room for mechanisms, examples, and tradeoffs; it is
not a mandatory minimum duration or a sequence of stretched short-video scripts.

## Build a progression of questions

Open with the concrete capability or problem and why it matters. State what the
viewer will be able to explain or decide. Show the real actors and goal early;
avoid a long title sequence, generic stakes, or a vocabulary list without context.

Outline chapters by questions, not just topic labels. For each, record the
starting mental model, the demonstration that changes it, the resulting answer,
and why the next question now matters. One example can progress from normal use
to a new constraint, a failure, and a tradeoff. Do not invent a failure or delay
basic information merely to manufacture suspense.

For example, a sourced cache-aside lesson can ask how an absent key is retrieved,
why the next request avoids the database, and what happens when the original data
changes. Each return to the diagram adds a consequence. State the assumptions of
any chosen expiration or invalidation policy; the illustration is not a claim
that every cache behaves that way.

Use chapter recaps to settle a question and connect it to the next one. Remove
repeated introductions and summaries that add no new distinction. If the brief
sets a duration, edit scope and wording; do not stretch approved speech or insert
empty holds to make the runtime look long-form.

## Make the mechanism visible

For each explanatory beat, record the viewer's question, current state, trigger,
visible action, resulting state, and what the viewer should infer. Tie the change
to the spoken concept. A labeled arrow establishes direction; a pulse alone does
not show what was transferred, compared, stored, rejected, or lost.

Show the relevant content and retain intermediate states. A copy keeps its source;
a queue gains or loses an item; a comparison presents the compared values and its
result. Do not claim internal state changes from decorative lights alone. Use
small readable identifiers and values, and label illustrative data as such.

Choose the view that exposes the current mechanism. A topology explains who can
talk to whom; a timeline shows order; a workbench exposes a value transformation;
a side-by-side view shows why two outcomes differ. Keep stable identities and
anchors across views. Do not force an arbitrary quota of layouts or change views
so often that the viewer must repeatedly reconstruct the system.

For a cache-fill beat, show an empty slot, the requested key, the returned value,
and the newly stored copy while the database retains the original. Later show
the stale/current values together when teaching freshness. A renamed pair of
boxes with another moving dot does not demonstrate either lesson. For a different
mechanism, author its own state and visual grammar rather than reuse that scene.

Use the 16:9 width for relationships, readable comparisons, and context. Do not
stretch portrait artwork or leave a narrow portrait column in an otherwise empty
wide frame. Keep captions outside the diagram, and use restrained depth without
tilting text or obscuring intermediate values. The starter is a mechanics example;
an episode needs authored scenes beyond its generic cards.

## Pace around understanding

Reveal the inputs, perform the action, then hold its consequence long enough to
read it. Give dense new concepts more time and familiar operations less. Measure
from native speech and actual playback. Avoid universal cut intervals, constant
motion, and fast montage as substitutes for editorial decisions.

A quiet teaching hold is useful when the viewer needs to compare or read. A hold
is dead time when the narration has moved on and the picture contributes nothing.
Reduce simultaneous novelty: introduce a new term, view, and operation in a
readable sequence rather than all at once. Remove narration that merely reads
labels when the viewer already has the information.

Choose the representative voiced sample from the core explanation. Include a
complete state change and consequence plus a transition or comparison that tests
the planned visual approach. The easiest intro cannot validate the hard middle.
In the full native-paced animatic, check that later chapters add understanding
and that repetition, dense explanation, and transitions remain readable.

## Repair a boring cut with evidence

Inspect the actual cut or available temporal evidence. Name the scene/timecode,
what is repeated or missing, its effect on understanding, and the smallest useful
repair. Distinguish an unclear mechanism, an unchanged picture under new narration,
redundant script, monotonous timing, and weak visual hierarchy. If no video is
available, identify these as risks to test, not an observed cause of boredom.

Recheck the affected interval and its neighbors through the existing bounded
review loop. Technical correctness and attractive art cannot pass an editorial
finding. Do not respond to boredom by adding arbitrary cuts, sounds, particles,
or faster voice. Keep actual playback/listening limits explicit; sampled stills
cannot establish perceived pace or engagement.
